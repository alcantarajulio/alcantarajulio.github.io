A hospital, a soft drink factory and a law firm look like they share nothing. All three
take inputs, transform something, and deliver a result to someone, and that similarity is
what lets you study them with the same toolkit.

## Every organisation is an operation

The basic model has three parts. Inputs, covering material, information, people,
facilities and equipment. A transformation process. And outputs, which are products,
services, or the mixture of the two that characterises nearly everything today.

What usually escapes notice is that the split between product and service stopped being
useful. The car manufacturer sells a car and financing and servicing. The restaurant
delivers food and hospitality. The productive question is not which of the two the
organisation does, but the proportion and what each part demands of the operation.

Operations management covers the decisions that make that transformation happen
predictably and at an acceptable cost. It is the function consuming most of the people and
most of the capital in most organisations, and the least discussed on a technology course.

## The four dimensions

Two operations in the same sector can demand opposite management, and four dimensions
explain why.

Volume is how much of the same thing gets produced. High volume permits repetition,
specialisation and automation, and unit cost falls. Low volume depends on people capable
of doing different things, and unit cost rises.

Variety is how many different things the operation delivers. High variety demands
flexibility and complicates planning. Low variety allows standardising everything.

Variation in demand is how much it swings over time. Stable demand lets you run near
capacity. Seasonal demand forces either idle capacity in the trough or lost sales in the
peak, and both options cost.

Visibility is how much of the process the customer sees. A high-visibility operation has
the customer inside it, which imposes a short tolerance for waiting and demands
interpersonal skill from whoever executes. A low-visibility operation can optimise without
an audience.

Placing an operation along these four usually explains decisions that looked arbitrary,
and that is the model's use.

## Operations strategy

Operations strategy is the bridge between what the organisation wants to be in the market
and what the operation needs to know how to do.

The translation runs both ways. Top down, business strategy sets priorities: competing on
price needs low cost, competing on customisation needs flexibility. Bottom up, what the
operation already does well opens or closes strategic options, and ignoring that produces
commercial promises the operation fails to deliver.

There is also market pressure, which sets what is acceptable to stay in the game, and
resource pressure, which sets what is possible with what exists.

## Deciding where to compete

An operation is not excellent at everything at once, and most management error comes from
pretending otherwise. Choosing means accepting being average at something.

The low-cost airline is the teaching example because the choice is visible: it gives up
comfort, central airports and rebooking flexibility, and in exchange reaches a cost the
others cannot. Every sacrifice supports the price.

The symmetrical error is the organisation promising the best product, the cheapest, the
fastest and the most customised. It did not choose, so the operation chooses for it,
usually along the path of least effort.

## In practice

Pick two operations you know closely, in the same sector. Two sandwich shops near the
university work well, one a chain and one independent.

Place both along the four dimensions, with one line of justification per position. Where do
they diverge?

Then list three visible decisions each has made: the size of the menu, whether you order at
the counter or the table, whether stock sits in view. Tie each decision to a position on
the dimensions. Most will explain themselves.

Finally, identify which of the five priorities each chose to compete on, and what it
accepted losing for that. If you cannot name the sacrifice, either the operation did not
choose, or you have not yet understood the choice.
