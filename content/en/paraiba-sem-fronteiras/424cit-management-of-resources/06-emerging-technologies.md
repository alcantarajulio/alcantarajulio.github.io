Every generation of managers faces the same question under a different name. This chapter
covers how to think about it without depending on picking the winning technology.

## Emerging and disruptive are not synonyms

An emerging technology sits at the start of the maturity curve. It could become anything,
including nothing.

A disruptive technology is a narrower and more useful concept. It describes an innovation
entering at the bottom of the market, performing worse than the standard on the dimensions
current customers value and better on some other: price, simplicity, accessibility. It
serves whoever was badly served or outside the market, improves over time, and climbs until
it serves the main customer too.

Most emerging technology is not disruptive but sustaining: it improves the existing product
along dimensions that already matter. Confusing the two leads to defending against the wrong
attack.

## Why the incumbent loses

The counterintuitive part of the theory is that the incumbent usually loses while making
correct decisions.

It asks its best customers what they want, and they do not want the inferior version. It
compares the margin of the new market against the current one, and the new one is worse. It
allocates resource by expected return, and the return from a small market is small.

Every step is rational and the set is fatal, because the small market grows and the inferior
technology improves faster than customer requirements rise. When the crossover arrives,
reacting demands a capability the organisation spent ten years not building.

The management implication is that reacting to disruption through conventional financial
analysis always yields the same answer, and that organisations which survive usually
separate the new bet from the structure that would kill it.

## Evaluating what has not matured

Without history and without an installed base, the usual instruments fail. Three questions
survive.

What constraint does this technology remove? Any technology with real effect takes one
specific limitation off the table: distance, unit cost, waiting time, the need for a
specialist. If you cannot name the constraint, you are probably looking at a solution
hunting for a problem.

Who already uses it and why? Early adoption in a niche is a more reliable signal than a
market projection. The niche shows where the removed constraint genuinely hurt.

And what has to be true for it to work at scale? Writing those conditions turns an opinion
into a testable hypothesis, and some of them you can check today.

## Extended reality as a case study

Virtual, augmented and mixed reality serve as a good example because they have already been
through two cycles of expectation and disappointment.

They remove a clear constraint: needing to be physically in front of something to manipulate
it or understand it spatially. Where that constraint is expensive, surgical training,
maintenance of complex equipment, architectural design, adoption happened and held. Where it
is not, adoption rose and fell alongside the expectation.

That is the pattern to hunt for in any new technology, and it is worth more than the
trend list of the year.

## The cost of waiting and the cost of arriving early

Two symmetrical errors exist, and the second gets discussed more than the first.

Adopting too early costs in immature products, vendors that disappear, integrations that
change, and internal credibility spent.

Adopting too late costs in capability never built. Technology does not arrive ready the
night before: the value comes from accumulated data, an adjusted process, and a team that
learned, and all three take time.

The practical way out is usually the small reversible bet, sized to learn rather than to
transform, with a stopping criterion declared beforehand, as in the risk chapter.

## In practice

Pick a technology currently riding high.

Write which constraint it removes, in one sentence, without using the technology's name.

Find two real production uses, with an identifiable organisation, and describe what was
particular about that niche.

List three conditions that have to be true for it to work at scale, and mark which ones you
can test now.

Finally, design the small bet: what you would do with limited resource over three months,
what you would learn, and what would make you abandon it.
