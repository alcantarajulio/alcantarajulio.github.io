Earlier chapters covered running the operation. This one covers what technology does to
it: where it enters, what it replaces, what it creates, and who feels the effect first.

## The layers of use

Organisations use technology in layers that accumulate, and recognising them prevents the
vague conversation about digital transformation.

The record layer only stores what already happened: the invoice that was paper became a
file. It reduces cost and error and changes no process.

The automation layer executes what a person executed. It changes the process and displaces
work.

The coordination layer connects parts that previously talked by phone and spreadsheet. It
produces the largest gain and fails most often in deployment, because it requires agreement
between departments.

And the decision layer uses accumulated data to choose, recommend or forecast. It is the
most recent in most organisations and the most dependent on the quality of the layers
below, which explains a good share of frustrated projects: nobody forecasts well on data
nobody cleaned.

## What automation actually does

Public debate treats automation as replacing people with machines, and what gets observed
in operations is more fragmented.

Automation replaces tasks rather than occupations. A job is a bundle of tasks, and
technology usually takes the most repetitive and predictable ones and leaves the rest. The
immediate effect recomposes the job rather than ending it.

That produces three consequences management has to anticipate. First, the skill required
changes, sometimes upward and sometimes downward, and not always for the same person.
Second, the remaining work tends to be the harder part, since the easy part got automated,
which raises the cognitive load on whoever stayed. Third, supervising an automated system
is a new task, badly paid and badly designed in most cases.

## The effects that arrive later

Every technological change generates second-order effects that were never in the project's
justification.

Remote work is the most studied recent example. The technology enabling it existed
beforehand, and mass adoption changed facility costs, performance evaluation, expectations
of availability, and the boundary between home and work. None of those items appeared in
the business case for buying videoconference licences.

It is also worth watching who wins and who loses inside the organisation, because the
distribution is rarely even. A system giving visibility to a manager can remove autonomy
from whoever executes, and the resistance appearing at deployment is usually an accurate
reading of that shift in power rather than a misunderstanding.

## Measuring the effect

Honest evaluation of a technology project has to compare against the previous situation,
and most evaluations skip it because nobody measured beforehand.

The rule of thumb records the baseline before touching anything: how long the process took,
how many errors it produced, what it cost, how many people it involved. Without that record,
the later evaluation becomes a contest of impressions, and the impression of whoever
championed the project usually wins.

Measure what was not in the objective too: time spent feeding the new system, rework during
the transition, support tickets. The net gain lives in the difference.

## In practice

Pick a process you watched get computerised, at university or at work.

Describe how it worked before, in steps, with who did each one.

Describe how it works now, in the same terms.

Identify which of the four layers the change happened in: record, automation, coordination
or decision.

List what improved and what got worse, and force yourself to fill both sides. If you find
nothing that got worse, ask someone who executes the process rather than someone who manages
it.

Finally, write down which three measures you would have collected beforehand, had you known
the change was coming.
