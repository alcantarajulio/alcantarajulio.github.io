"The operation is doing well" carries no content until you say well at what. This chapter
covers the five possible answers, the fact that they compete with each other, and the set
of legal obligations restricting all of them.

## The five objectives

Quality is doing it right. It reduces cost and raises dependability at the same time,
which makes it the only objective that helps all the others, and that is why it heads
every list.

Speed is the time between the order and the delivery. It pleases the customer and also
reduces internal risk, since it shortens the horizon the operation has to forecast.

Dependability is delivering when you promised. It usually matters more than speed, and
transport makes that clear: a bus taking forty minutes that always arrives on time
organises a rider's life better than one taking thirty that varies twenty either way.

Flexibility is the ability to change. It has four faces worth separating: changing the
product, changing the mix, changing the volume, and changing the delivery date.

And cost is a consequence of the other four. No organisation competes on cost alone
without having settled something first, because low cost with poor quality becomes a
compulsory discount later.

## The conflicts between them

The point exams ask about and practice confirms is that these objectives compete for
resource.

Raising flexibility requires idle capacity or fast changeover, and raises cost. Raising
speed beyond a point requires stock built in advance or extra capacity, and raises cost.
Cutting cost by removing slack destroys dependability at the first abnormality.

Quality is the partial exception, since preventing a defect costs less than correcting it.
Even so, raising quality above what the customer perceives is spending without return.

Managing an operation means choosing the combination, declaring the choice, and living
with it. Measuring all five with no hierarchy between them produces a handsome dashboard
and a paralysed decision.

## Facilities

Facilities management looks after the physical space and the services keeping it working:
maintenance, cleaning, energy, physical security, access control.

It is invisible when it works and the first thing blamed when it fails, which produces a
pattern of underinvestment. Preventive maintenance shows up in no outcome indicator, and
corrective maintenance shows up in all of them.

Two decisions structure the area. The first is what to keep in house and what to
outsource, which depends on how specific the service is and how much risk its interruption
carries. The second is the maintenance model: corrective, which waits for the breakage;
preventive, which replaces on a calendar; or predictive, which monitors condition and
intervenes when the data says so. The third costs more to set up and less to run, and
depends on sensors and history.

## The legal frame

Every operation runs inside a set of obligations that hold regardless of the chosen
strategy, and not knowing them is no defence.

The areas the module covers repeat across jurisdictions under different names:
occupational health and safety, which the next chapter details; personal data protection,
the GDPR in Europe and the LGPD in Brazil, close enough in structure to study together;
consumer law; employment relations; and environmental obligations.

The most common management error here treats compliance as a project with an end date. It
is a state the operation maintains, and the evidence of that maintenance, training records,
inspection reports, access logs, is what separates the diligent organisation from the one
that merely claims to be.

## In practice

Return to the operation you analysed in the previous chapter.

Rank the five performance objectives by the importance that operation actually gives each,
judging by what it does rather than what it says. Justify the first and the last position.

Then find a real conflict: a decision where it sacrificed one objective for another.
Describe what was traded.

Next, propose an indicator for each of the top two objectives in your ranking. It has to be
measurable with data the operation already holds or can collect without a new project. That
constraint is the exercise.

Finally, list three legal obligations applying to that operation and say what evidence it
would need to produce to demonstrate each.
