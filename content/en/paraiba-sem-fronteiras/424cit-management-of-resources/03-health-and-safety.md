This is the chapter computing students tend to skip, and it carries the most direct
consequence. Other management decisions cost money when wrong. This one costs somebody
their body.

## The principle organising everything

Occupational health and safety law varies between countries and converges on one
principle: the employer owes a duty of care to whoever works for them and to whoever is
affected by the activity, and that duty is active.

Active means the obligation is not to react to the accident but to look for what could
cause one before it happens. And it means the duty reaches visitors, contractors and the
public, not only registered employees.

Most legislation carries an important qualification: the requirement is to reduce risk so
far as is reasonably practicable. That allows proportionality, since no activity carries
zero risk, while placing the burden on the organisation to show it weighed cost against
risk honestly.

## Hazard and risk

The two terms get used interchangeably in conversation and mean different things in the
discipline.

A hazard is the thing with potential to cause harm: the ladder, the chemical, the cable
across the floor, the twelve-hour shift. Risk is the chance that the hazard harms
somebody, combined with how severe the harm would be.

The distinction is operational. You do not eliminate the hazard of a ladder existing. You
reduce the risk by controlling who climbs it, with what, and in what condition.

## The five-step assessment

The standard method has five stages, and it works by being simple enough that somebody
actually uses it.

First, identify the hazards, by walking the place, asking whoever works there, and reading
the history of incidents and near misses.

Second, identify who could be harmed and how. This step surfaces groups a generic
assessment misses: the lone worker, the person doing maintenance out of hours, the pregnant
worker, the visitor who does not know the building.

Third, evaluate the risk and decide the controls, which means comparing what already exists
against what it would be reasonable to add.

Fourth, record it. The record is not a formality: it is the evidence the assessment
happened, and it is what the organisation produces when somebody asks.

Fifth, review. Any change of process, equipment or team invalidates part of the previous
assessment.

## The hierarchy of controls

Not every control is worth the same, and there is an order of preference management tends
to invert, because inverting it is cheaper in the short run.

Eliminating the hazard is best, and it means no longer doing that thing that way.
Substituting something less dangerous comes next. Then engineering controls, separating the
person from the hazard with a barrier, extraction, or machine guarding. Then administrative
controls, changing procedure, rotation and training. And last, personal protective
equipment.

The order matters because effectiveness falls as you descend. Elimination depends on nobody
remembering. Personal equipment depends on the person wearing it correctly every shift, and
protects only whoever wears it.

An organisation answering every risk with training and personal equipment has chosen the
bottom of the hierarchy, and usually chose on cost.

## Emergency

An emergency plan exists for the moment when nobody can think. It has to cover evacuation
with a route and an assembly point, who has authority to stop the operation, first aid, and
communication with external services.

The component that fails is the drill. A plan never tested has a locked door, a route
through the store room, and a contact list three years old. The test is what turns a
document into a capability.

## In practice

Run a real risk assessment, on the university computing lab or wherever you work.

Walk the space and list six hazards. Force yourself to include at least two that are not
physical: workload, posture, isolation, noise.

For each, write who could be harmed and how, paying attention to the groups a generic
assessment misses.

Rate each risk on probability and severity, with the scale defined beforehand.

For the top three, propose a control and say which level of the hierarchy it sits at. If
all three are training or personal equipment, go back and try to climb.

Finally, write the review date and what would trigger a review sooner.
