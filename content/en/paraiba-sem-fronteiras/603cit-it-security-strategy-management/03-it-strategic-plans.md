Analysis that never becomes a plan is conversation. The previous chapter showed where
technology creates advantage; this one covers writing that down so somebody approves it,
funds it and follows it.

## What a plan has to answer

A useful IT strategic plan answers five questions, and most bad plans fail by omitting the
fourth and the fifth.

Where we are today, with honesty about the technical debt and about what is broken. Where
we want to arrive, expressed as capability rather than product. Why that direction, tied
explicitly to a business objective. How we get there, in sequence and with dependencies.
And how we will know we arrived, as an indicator somebody can measure.

The fifth question separates a plan from an intention. "Modernise the infrastructure" is
not measurable. "Cut environment provisioning from ten days to one" is.

## Size matters, and smaller usually wins

One format became standard because it works: the strategy on a page. It forces
prioritisation, since one page holds no fifteen initiatives, and it forces clarity, since
there is no room for a defensive paragraph.

The long document has a place as an annex, for whoever needs the detail. What circulates,
what gets read in a board meeting and what the team can remember is the page.

The rule of thumb: if you cannot explain the plan in two minutes to someone outside IT, it
is not ready, and the audience is not the problem.

## A roadmap is not a schedule

The two get confused and serve different purposes.

A schedule promises dates for known deliverables. It works inside a project and lies when
applied to a three-year horizon, because nobody knows what happens in year three.

A roadmap organises direction and sequence. It shows what comes first by dependency, what
can run in parallel, and groups work into horizons of decreasing confidence: next quarter
is commitment, next year is intent, beyond that is direction.

Presenting direction with the precision of a schedule creates a credibility debt you repay
at the next review.

## Linking objectives to measures

The strategy map and the balanced scorecard exist to solve a specific problem:
organisations measure what is easy to measure, and what is easy to measure in IT is
activity rather than outcome.

Counting closed tickets measures motion. Measuring downtime on the billing system measures
consequence. The structure forces you to walk the chain, from learning and growth through
internal process, customer and financial result, asking at each step how the level below
sustains the one above.

The value of the exercise sits less in the final dashboard and more in the conversation it
provokes, because that is where the initiatives sustaining nothing come to light.

## Portfolio and the decision to stop

Planning is deciding what not to do, and that is the part organisations avoid.

Portfolio management looks at initiatives together and distributes resource between
keeping what exists, improving what exists, and creating what is new. The proportion
between the three is a strategic choice: put everything into maintenance and you never
change level; put everything into innovation and the base crumbles.

What is almost always missing is a stopping criterion. A project without a declared
condition for cancellation survives on inertia, consuming the resource the next initiative
would need. Writing at the start what would make you cancel is cheap; writing it halfway
through is impossible.

## In practice

Write the IT strategy of a real organisation on one page, and respect the limit.

Start with the diagnosis in three sentences. Write the destination as capability, with
three items at most. Tie each item to a business objective with one sentence of
justification. Place the three on a roadmap with three horizons, marking what is commitment
and what is direction. Define one indicator per item, with today's value and the intended
one.

Then do the uncomfortable exercise: list what the organisation does today that your plan
implies stopping. If that list is empty, you wrote a budget request rather than a strategy.
