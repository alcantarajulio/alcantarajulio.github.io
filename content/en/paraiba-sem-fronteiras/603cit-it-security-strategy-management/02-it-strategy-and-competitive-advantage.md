Two companies in the same sector buy the same software from the same vendor. One gains
market share with it and the other just spends. The difference is not in the software,
and working out where it does sit is the subject of this chapter.

## Commodity and differentiator

Not all technology creates advantage. Most of it is infrastructure: necessary to operate,
available to any competitor, and incapable of distinguishing whoever uses it. Email was a
differentiator in 1995 and today it is plumbing.

The test is imitability. If a competitor buys the same thing tomorrow, you hold parity
rather than advantage. Advantage appears when technology combines with something your
organisation has and the competitor does not: the data you accumulated, the process you
designed around it, the competence of the team running it.

That repositions the investment question. For infrastructure technology, the goal is cost
and reliability, and buying off the shelf usually wins. For differentiating technology,
the goal is unique capability, and building starts to make sense.

## Where technology acts on competition

The five forces model describes competitive pressure in a sector, and each force has a
technological reading.

Technology moves the barrier to entry in both directions: it lowers the barrier when it
lets a newcomer operate without the incumbent's physical footprint, and raises it when it
creates network effects the newcomer cannot replicate.

It shifts supplier power when it reduces the cost of switching suppliers, and buyer power
when price information becomes available to everyone.

And it creates substitutes, the most dangerous force because it arrives from outside the
sector. Whoever ran video rental shops did not lose to another video rental shop.

The value chain completes the picture by looking inward. It separates activities that
produce value from those that support the operation, and forces the question of which one
the technology actually improves. Investment in a support activity reduces cost.
Investment in a primary activity can change the offer.

## Alignment

The term appears in every exam and gets repeated without content. Alignment means the IT
strategy derives from the business strategy and informs it back.

The derivation is the obvious half: if the business decided to grow by acquisition, IT
needs integration and standardisation. The feedback is the forgotten half: when IT spots a
capability that opens a strategic option, it needs a route to carry that into the
decision, and organisations where IT reports three levels down have no such route.

The classic symptom of misalignment is a portfolio nobody can justify. You ask why a
project exists and the answer is historical rather than strategic.

## Governance

Governance answers who decides what. It is not bureaucracy in itself, though it becomes
that when badly designed.

Three arrangements exist, and each solves one problem while creating another. Centralised
standardises, reduces cost, and frustrates the business unit with a legitimately different
need. Decentralised serves each area well and produces twenty incompatible systems nobody
can integrate later. Federated attempts the middle: infrastructure and policy at the
centre, applications at the edge, with a boundary that has to be made explicit, because an
implicit boundary generates permanent conflict.

## In practice

Take an organisation you know, and the university itself will do.

List five technology systems or services it uses. For each, answer whether a competitor
could buy the same thing tomorrow. The ones they could are infrastructure, and the question
about them is cost. The ones they could not are candidate differentiators, and it is worth
understanding why.

Then pick one candidate and write down what sustains the difference: the data, the process,
the team, or the combination. If you cannot write it, it probably is not a differentiator.

Finally, test alignment. Pick a technology project underway and ask whoever runs it which
business objective it serves. If the answer takes a while, you found the symptom.
