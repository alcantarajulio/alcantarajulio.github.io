The system went live on time, on budget, with every function in the specification. Six
months later half the users are still on the old spreadsheet. By the project's metric it
succeeded. By any metric that matters it did not.

## Why delivered systems fail

The usual answer blames resistance to change, and it is usually wrong. A user who returns
to the spreadsheet generally has a reason: the new system asks for eight clicks where the
spreadsheet asked for two, or it fails to cover the case that makes up half their work, or
they distrust the numbers it shows.

Treating that as a behaviour problem leads to training and an announcement. Treating it as
a design problem leads to asking what the system demands of the user and what it returns.

## The acceptance model

The technology acceptance model proposes that the intention to use a system depends on two
perceptions.

Perceived usefulness is how far the person believes the system improves their performance.
Perceived ease of use is how far they believe using it costs little effort. The second
also feeds the first, since a difficult system looks less useful.

The detail most summaries lose is the word perceived. The model measures belief about the
system rather than the system. An objectively better system with a bad internal reputation
faces the same problem as a bad system, and the intervention differs in each case.

Later extensions added social norm, meaning what colleagues do, and facilitating
conditions, meaning whether support and infrastructure exist. Those two explain why a pilot
that succeeded with one team fails to reproduce with the next.

## Measuring system success

A complementary model breaks success into dimensions that get confused: system quality,
the quality of the information it produces, the quality of the support service, actual
use, user satisfaction, and the benefit that results.

The model's utility lies in the relationships between the dimensions. Poor information
quality destroys satisfaction even on a technically impeccable system, which is the most
common outcome of a botched data migration. Poor support destroys use even on a good
system.

That gives an actionable diagnosis: when adoption is low, you have six places to look
rather than one generic conclusion.

## The expectation cycle

New technology follows a reasonably predictable curve of public expectation. It rises fast
after the first visible case, peaks where the promise far exceeds delivery, falls when the
early projects fail, and climbs slowly again to a plateau where use is real and less
reported.

The curve's practical value is a calendar. Buying at the peak means paying a premium for an
immature product and becoming a failure case. Buying in the trough means contradicting the
internal perception that the thing was already debunked, and it is usually where the best
ratio of cost to maturity sits.

The curve describes perception rather than technical capability, which is why it misreads
technologies that climb slowly without ever peaking.

## Before deciding

A short set of questions prevents most adoption mistakes.

What problem does this solve, described without naming the technology? Who will use it, and
did that person take part in the evaluation? What is the total cost, including migration,
training and year two? What happens if the vendor disappears, and what does leaving cost?
And what is the criterion for calling the adoption a failure, defined before starting?

The last one is the most avoided and the most useful, for the same reason as the stopping
criterion in the previous chapter.

## In practice

Pick a system you use and dislike using.

Split your reaction into perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, and write a
sentence on each. Then walk the six dimensions of the success model and locate the main
defect: the system, the information, the support, or none of the three, the problem being
that it solves a problem you do not have.

Then do the same with a system you like, because contrasting the two reveals more than
analysing one.

Finally, write the five decision questions for a technology you recently saw someone
propose, and try to answer the one about year two honestly.
